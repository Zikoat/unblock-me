How it works

  1. Candidates. The sliding blocks nearest the last target (in the 8×8 box round it), at most 12, nearest first; not an earlier red block, nor one on its track.
  2. Push search (pushes, src/goal-fast.ts). Each candidate is pushed both ways one cell at a time. Before each step, a small backwards search clears the cell ahead: the block in it slides away, which may need other blocks out of its way first (at most moves a cell, depth-first with prunings). A push stops when a cell can't be cleared, or its share of the work budget is spent ( ticks for all pushes, each cell costing ). The moves so far prove its cell can be reached.
  3. Rough measure. Every cell reached is a candidate target. Its proof is shortened (moves folded together where the sequence still plays), and A* gets boards to find its fewest moves: exact if it finishes, else only a lower bound.
  4. Ranking. Nearest the ramp's aim first; then by difficulty for the distance: score / distance0.6, halved for each block on the red block's own track (score = fewest moves × log₂ of the average moves to choose from); then the shorter proof.
  5. Exact measure (measure, src/difficulty.ts) for the best , with up to boards. The search runs in a window round the red block's run (2 cells round, widened to fit the proof), where only the blocks a chain of lanes links to the red block or the proof may slide; everything else stands still. First a greedy pass (weight 4 on the lower bound) finds some solution fast; then plain A* proves nothing shorter exists, skipping boards that can't beat it. A shorter solution replaces the push's proof.
  6. The goal. The finalists are ranked again with their exact measures; the best is the goal, its solution checked to replay on the real board.

What a goal is optimised for

A goal should be hard (many moves, many choices on the way) but near (the player shouldn't travel, and a long push is mostly sliding). The candidates are filtered, then sorted by a key of four parts, each breaking the ties of the one before:

rank: 1. |M − aim| smaller first (during the ramp only) 2. balance bigger first 3. proof length shorter first 4. d nearer first balance = score / d^0.6 × 0.5^n score = M × log₂ b
M: fewest moves
The fewest moves that solve it (one block sliding any distance is one move), by A* in a window round the red block's run; a lower bound when the search stops first. The player's moves are compared with it for medals.
b: choices
The legal moves on an average board the search expanded: how many ways the player could go at each step (counted in the window, so mostly blocks that matter).
score
M × log₂ b: the size of the tree of move sequences as long as the shortest solution, in bits; each point doubles it. 7 moves at 16 choices is 28.
d: distance
Cells the red block travels to its target, divided out as d0.6: a goal twice as far needs a score about 1.5× higher to win, so a near goal nearly as hard wins. (A power of 0 would nearly always take the furthest cell; 1 would rank by score per cell.) Tuned on play data (play-analysis).
n: blocks on the track
Blocks lying the same way in the red block's own lane, between it and the target: they can only be pushed ahead of it, tedious rather than hard. Each halves the balance (blocks-on-track).
aim
The first 30 world goals ramp up: goal k aims at round(4 + 6k / 30) fewest moves, 4 for the first and 10 for the 30th. During the ramp the candidates nearest the aim always win; after it there is no aim (world-ramp).
Near the last target
Not scored but a filter: only blocks in the 8×8 box round the last target can be the red block (nearest first, at most 12), so the next goal starts within about 4 cells of where the last one ended.
Rules
Not an earlier red block, nor a block on its row (horizontal) or column (vertical); the target never on an earlier target's cells; the solution must replay on the real board.
