Walls and repeated moves

Does a good goal need blocks moved more than once, and do walls (fixed 1×1 cells) in the generated world make that more common? Fresh boards are swept over two things: the wall share (the share of cells that are walls) and the block density (the layout's tries per 4×4 chunk; the game uses 12). Today's maker is asked for a goal round the origin, as for the world's first, at effort , with no ramp aim. Every number comes from the maker's own proof: no other search for a shorter one.

What it shows

  • The gut feeling holds for long goals, not for walls. Blocks moved twice or more come with long proofs: about 90% of proofs over 15 moves have one, about a third of those of 11-15, under 10% of shorter ones, whatever the walls. Block density is what makes proofs long: with no walls the share of goals with a re-moved block goes from 4% at density 6 to 64% at 16.
  • Random walls make re-moved blocks rarer: from 35% of goals with no walls to 10% at 15% walls (all densities), and goals with a directed blocker cycle from 10% to 4%. The goals get shorter (12.6 moves at the mean to 6.7), with fewer decisions and a shorter run for red.
  • Not because of the selection. Among all the candidates the maker weighs, 11% have a re-moved block with no walls; among its 3 finalists 28%; among the goals it picks 43%. The same rise holds at every wall share (15% walls: 4%, 8%, 12%). At the same proof length, candidates with a re-moved block reach the finals up to 5 times as often as the rest (about as often for long candidates at 8-15% walls), the Judge's fitness for them is 1.2 to 2 times higher (one exception: 16+ moves at 15% walls, with only 5 candidates on the other side), and their decision count is about the same (−0.8 to +0.7). The rank and the Judge favour them or are even; neither the decision count's stopping rule nor any other term holds them back.
  • It is the pool. Walls shrink what the push search can find, since a push stops at a wall: 131 candidates a board with no walls, 41 at 15%; the mean candidate goes from 7.4 to 4.1 moves, each board's longest from 21 to 12, and candidates of 11 or more moves from 23% to 5%. The pick still takes the long ones (62% of chosen goals have 11+ moves with no walls, 11% at 15%), but there are fewer to take.
  • At the same proof length walls do not lower the share; if anything they raise it a little (proofs of 7-10 moves: 4% with none, 7-9% from 6% walls; candidates of 11-15 moves: 31% with none, 48% at 15%). So a wall makes a goal of a given length more order-bound, but walls placed at random cut the long goals out of reach.
  • Effort 2 (more candidates, wider measures; 60 boards a cell at 4 wall shares × densities 8, 12, 16) shows the same trend, so it is not effort 0's narrow search alone.
  • Every board gave a legal goal, at every wall share and density. Up to about 4% walls little changes at the game's density, so the game's walls slider starts there.
  • Time: the maker is 99% of it, and the measure's lower bound (window.ts) is 61-66% of the maker, the rest of A* 16-18%; by step, the rough measure of every candidate takes about two thirds. Walls make all of it cheaper (212 ms a board with none, 32 at 15%).

Candidates or selection?

Do re-moved blocks get rarer with walls because the maker's selection (its rank, then the Judge's fitness among its finalists) passes them over, or because walls leave fewer of them among the goals it can find at all? Every candidate the maker weighs was recorded with its proof and the Judge's terms (walls-candidates.ts: boards at block density ). The maker measures every candidate roughly, ranks them, measures the best 3 (its finalists) exactly, and picks one by the Judge's fitness.

Where the time goes

First the whole sweep, per board: the maker against everything else the experiment runs. Then inside the maker, from a CPU profile of the candidate runs (each sample counted for the deepest function on its stack that names a part; the experiment's own Judge calls on every candidate are kept apart).

The derived numbers

  • Re-moved block: a block moves 2 or more times in the proof. Exact for the proof; another shortest solution might move blocks fewer times (not searched).
  • Back-and-forth: the proof returns to a block it already left (with back-to-back slides of one block merged, every repeat is a return); reversal: a block slides back the way it came at some point. Both from the proof alone.
  • Blocker graph (approximation): an edge A → B when B's slide sweeps a cell A was the last block to leave, from the proof's order alone (not from which blocks truly had to move: a block that left a cell for some other reason still counts). Cyclomatic complexity = edges − nodes + components over the blocks moved, directed edges counted once each (A → B and B → A both count, so a pair that must take turns gives 1). Directed cycle: some block is, through the chain, in its own way.
  • Detour: the proof's moves per cell of red's straight distance (start to target), median.
  • Fewest moves is the proof's length (the Judge's moves term, no Auditor): the maker's window measure finds it exact for the share shown as "exact"; elsewhere it is an upper bound.
  • Decisions and fitness are the Judge's (src/judge.ts) on the proof's length; maker time is the median of one process each, on the VM (2 processes on 2 cores).
  • Candidates: a candidate's proof is the rough measure's shortest where it found one, else the push search's; the finalists' and the chosen goal's are after the exact measure, so they can be shorter than as candidates.
