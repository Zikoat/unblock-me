Yes. This is a well-studied problem in AI planning, model checking, concurrency theory, and graph theory. There is even a branch of research that treats state spaces as geometric objects, where independent movements form continuous regions and interactions create obstacles or constraints.

The most relevant concepts are:

- Partial-order reduction (POR): Avoids exploring different orderings of independent actions.
- Factored state spaces / decoupled search: Represents independent components separately, rather than enumerating every combination.
- Directed topology and cubical complexes: Gives a geometric interpretation of independent movements and their interactions.
- Petri net unfoldings: Represents dependencies and concurrency without enumerating every possible action ordering.

Your idea is especially close to the intersection of the first three.

## 1. The geometry of independent movements

Imagine a puzzle with two movable boxes, A and B. For simplicity, each can make three independent moves.

A combined state is represented by a pair:

\\[ s=(a,b) \\]

where \\(a\\) describes the state of box A and \\(b\\) describes box B.

Instead of visualizing these states as an arbitrary graph, we can arrange them in a coordinate system: A's progress along the horizontal axis and B's along the vertical axis.

When the components are completely independent, their combined state graph is a Cartesian product:

\\[ G=G_A \mathbin{\square} G_B \\]

This creates a grid with many paths between the same two states. For example, moving A and then B reaches the same result as moving B and then A.

The important observation is that the different paths need not represent fundamentally different solutions. Their differences may be nothing more than the ordering of independent actions.

Mathematically, if two actions \\(a\\) and \\(b\\) commute at a state \\(s\\):

\\[ T_b(T_a(s))=T_a(T_b(s)) \\]

and both orders are legal, then the two paths form a commuting diamond.

This gives us a way to identify redundant search branches without pretending that all intermediate states are identical.

## 2. Visualizing where independence breaks down

The blocked combination removes four commuting squares. The paths on opposite sides of the resulting hole can no longer be transformed into each other using only legal swaps of independent moves. Whether a search must retain both depends on the goal and cost model.

The geometric interpretation is particularly interesting:

A commuting square can be treated as a filled-in surface, not just four edges. Two paths across that surface are equivalent under reordering. Interactions remove squares or introduce constraints, potentially producing holes, bottlenecks, and distinct classes of paths.

This is almost exactly the subject of [A Geometric View of Partial Order Reduction](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/19274418/Goubalt_Heindel_ET_AL_2013_A_Geometric_View_of_Partial_Order_Reduction.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com) by Goubault, Heindel and Mimram (2013). The paper uses directed geometric spaces to represent concurrent computations and compares geometric equivalence of paths with traditional partial-order reduction.&#x20;

## 3. Moving beyond pruning: compressing whole regions

There is a second, more powerful idea implicit in your question.

Suppose you have 10 moves for A and 10 moves for B, with each entity's moves internally ordered.

There are:

\\[ \binom{20}{10}=184\\,756 \\]

possible interleavings, even though their independent progress coordinates have only \\(11\times11=121\\) combinations.

Partial-order reduction can eliminate redundant interleavings. But it doesn't automatically eliminate the need to represent distinct combinations of local states.

For that, you want factored or decoupled search.

Instead of storing every state \\((a,b)\\) individually, represent an independent region symbolically as:

\\[ R=S_A\times S_B \\]

and explore the separate component graphs. Only when an interaction matters do you need to impose cross-component constraints or materialize particular combinations.

Conceptually, the visualization becomes a hierarchy: large compressed Cartesian-product regions linked through relatively small interaction boundaries. The solver can operate on those boundaries while solving local movement problems independently.

This connects directly to [Star-topology decoupled state space search](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000437021730173X?utm_source=chatgpt.com) (Gnad and Hoffmann, 2018). Their approach searches over central-factor transitions while tracking compatible states and costs of conditionally independent leaf factors.&#x20;

Yes. I would use Strong Stubborn Sets (SSS), a general partial-order reduction algorithm developed for automated planning. It applies naturally to Rush Hour / Unblock Me because every piece has its own position variable, and moves interfere only through collision and blocking constraints.

For your infinite-board variant, however, I would separate the problem into three reductions:

| Problem                                       | Technique                                    | Purpose                                                        |
| --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Infinitely many possible positions            | Symbolic intervals / event-based abstraction | Represent large ranges of positions without enumerating them   |
| Combinatorial explosion of independent pieces | Factored or decoupled search                 | Avoid materializing every combination of independent positions |
| Redundant orderings of moves                  | Strong Stubborn Sets                         | Avoid exploring permutations of independent moves              |

These techniques address different sources of complexity, and can be combined.

## 1. How Strong Stubborn Sets work

Suppose pieces A, B and C can all move, but only A and C influence whether the target piece can escape.

A normal search generates all moves of A, B and C. Strong stubborn sets instead identifies a subset of actions that is sufficient to preserve a solution, including an optimal one.

Conceptually, at each state:

```
T = actions forming a necessary goal-related set

repeat until T stops growing:
    for each action a in T:
        if a is enabled:
            add every enabled action that interferes with a
        else:
            add a necessary enabling set for a

expand only enabled actions in T
```

Here, the initial set must contain at least one action from every possible solution (a disjunctive action landmark). For Rush Hour, it could contain all actions that move the target piece to the exit.

If that action is blocked, we add the actions that can enable it. If those actions interfere with other actions, we include the interfering actions too.

The result is a dependency closure. Unrelated pieces do not have to be expanded at that state.

Importantly, the published SSS conditions preserve completeness and optimal solution cost, not just the existence of some solution.&#x20;

## 2. Detecting independent moves in Unblock Me

This is simpler than for arbitrary planning problems because the geometry gives you a natural dependency test.

Represent a move as:

\\[ m=(\text{piece},\text{from},\text{to}) \\]

For each move, compute the cells swept by that piece.

A conservative independence test is:

\\[ \operatorname{sweep}(A)\cap\operatorname{sweep}(B)=\varnothing \\]

for moves of distinct pieces.

When their swept regions don't intersect, neither move can prevent the other, and their order doesn't matter.

A more precise state-dependent test checks that both orders are executable and produce the same state:

\\[ A(B(s))=B(A(s)) \\]

That handles some moves whose swept regions overlap but that nevertheless commute.

However, independence of two currently available moves is not sufficient by itself to discard an arbitrary branch. You must also preserve the moves necessary to enable future goal-relevant actions. That's why SSS uses both interference and necessary enabling sets.

## 3. The infinite-board problem is separate

Consider a horizontal vehicle that can move to any integer coordinate, with no wall to stop it.

If any displacement counts as one move, that vehicle already has infinitely many successors.

POR does not fix this.

Instead, I'd introduce critical-position abstraction.

Suppose vehicle A can interact with B only when A occupies certain columns. Its possible positions might be partitioned into:

Position classes for vehicle A

Illustrative partition into interaction-relevant position classes.

The key insight is that the collision relationship between vehicles changes only at particular geometric boundaries.

For fixed-length, axis-aligned pieces, these boundaries arise from vehicle endpoints, intersecting movement lanes, walls, and goal conditions.

You could represent a potentially infinite collection of coordinates as a finite set of intervals and event positions.

This is promising, but the abstraction must be designed carefully: two coordinates that have identical immediate collision relationships are not necessarily interchangeable for future moves or shortest-path cost. A sound abstraction must preserve the relevant reachability and cost information.

If every one-cell displacement counts as a move, the abstraction also needs to retain the distance cost.

# Designing difficult Unblock Me puzzles using state-space search

The most promising approach is to combine graph-theoretic difficulty metrics, a model of human reasoning, and search-based procedural generation.

There is research directly addressing your question—not just general search theory, but specifically how the structure of the Rush Hour state-space graph affects human difficulty.

A central result is that shortest solution length is not a sufficient difficulty metric. Two puzzles requiring the same number of optimal moves can be dramatically different in how difficult they are for humans.

In What Determines Difficulty of Transport Puzzles? (Jarušek & Pelánek, 2011), the researchers studied human play across Rush Hour, Sokoban, and another transport puzzle. They found that state-space structure explains difficulty differences that static measures such as state-space size and optimal solution length cannot adequately capture.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.muni.cz\&sz=32)

Masarykova univerzita | MUNI+1



This suggests treating puzzle generation as a multi-objective optimization problem:

\\[ \boxed{ \text{Good puzzle} = \text{Solvability} +\text{Reasoning difficulty} -\text{Tedium} -\text{Unfair ambiguity} } \\]

This is a proposed design objective, not a published universal difficulty formula.

## 1. First, define difficulty at three different levels

| Level               | What it measures                                                 | How                                                      |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Solution complexity | Minimum work required to solve the puzzle                        | Shortest-path distance                                   |
| Search complexity   | How hard it is to discover a solution                            | Search expansions, misleading branches, dependency depth |
| Human difficulty    | How long players take, how often they make mistakes or get stuck | Simulated human search, then player validation           |

These are related but not interchangeable.

For example, a puzzle requiring 30 obvious moves might be easier than one requiring 12 moves in which the player must temporarily move the target away from the exit to unblock another piece.

For generation, I'd optimize primarily for human search complexity, constrained by a sensible range of solution lengths.

## 2. For small boards, start with exact reverse search

Suppose the board layout, pieces, orientations, and lengths are fixed. Let the states be vertices in a graph:

\\[ G=(V,E) \\]

Each edge is one legal slide. Assume moving a piece any legal distance counts as one move.

Let \\(W\\) be the set of solved configurations. Define:

\\[ d^\*(s)=\min\_{g\in W}\operatorname{dist}(s,g) \\]

You can compute \\(d^\*\\) for every solvable state using multi-source reverse BFS, starting simultaneously from all goal states. For reversible Rush Hour moves, ordinary BFS from the goals suffices.

This is a particularly useful result because it tells you the exact minimum moves from every position—not only the initial position.

It is also an established approach for generating Rush Hour puzzles. In On the Symbolic Computation of the Hardest Configurations of the Rush Hour Game (Collette, Raskin & Servais, 2006), researchers used binary decision diagrams (BDDs) and symbolic backward exploration to analyze roughly 36 billion configurations under their standard 6×6 model. Their hardest configuration required 93 moves.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://paperzz.com\&sz=32)

Paperzz+1



For your small-board prototype, you don't need BDDs initially.

### What the distance map tells you

Suppose a state has \\(d^\*(s)=12\\). Every legal action \\(a\\) can be classified according to its successor \\(s'\\):

| Distance of successor | Meaning                                 |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| \\(d^\*(s')=11\\)     | Optimal first move                      |
| \\(d^\*(s')=12\\)     | No reduction in optimal remaining moves |
| \\(d^\*(s')=13\\)     | Moves farther from a solution           |

This is a graph-theoretic definition of progress, not a visual one.

A move can push the target car physically away from the exit while still reducing \\(d^\*\\) from 12 to 11. Such a move is globally productive, even though it looks counterintuitive.

### Measuring how difficult it is to reposition one block

For a specific vehicle \\(i\\), suppose you want to move it into an interval of positions \\(I\\).

Define:

\\[ D_i(s,I)=\min\_{t:\\;p_i(t)\in I}\operatorname{dist}(s,t) \\]

Here, other vehicles are allowed to move along the way.

This gives you the minimum number of total vehicle moves needed to reposition vehicle \\(i\\), including moving its blockers.

You can compute this using BFS with the goal condition \\(p_i\in I\\). For example, if moving a vertical vehicle down by two spaces requires first repositioning four other vehicles, its difficulty is not merely the distance it slides.

But again, this is a measure of optimal work, not necessarily the human effort needed to discover the necessary sequence.

## 3. A difficulty metric derived from the state-space graph

One particularly useful approach is to model a player as a probabilistic search process navigating the graph.

Instead of assuming the player always chooses the optimal action, give each action a probability.

For a very simple player model:

\\[ \pi\_\beta(a\mid s) = \frac{\exp(-\beta d^\*(T(s,a)))} {\sum\_{b\in A(s)}\exp(-\beta d^\*(T(s,b)))} \\]

where:

- \\(T(s,a)\\) is the state reached by action \\(a\\).
- \\(A(s)\\) is the set of legal actions.
- \\(\beta=0\\) represents a uniformly random player.
- Larger \\(\beta\\) makes the player increasingly likely to select moves with lower true remaining distance.

The important quantity is then the expected number of moves the simulated player requires to find a solution:

\\[ H\_\pi(s)=1+\sum\_{a\in A(s)} \pi(a\mid s)H\_\pi(T(s,a)) \\]

with \\(H\_\pi(g)=0\\) for goal states.

For a small finite graph, this system of equations can be solved exactly when the policy reaches a goal with finite expected time. For larger graphs, estimate it using simulated playthroughs, with a move or time limit.

This quantity is the expected hitting time of the goal under the player policy.

Illustrative comparison: equal optimal length, different difficulty

Puzzle A: forgiving

Several alternatives converge toward progress

Puzzle B: misleading

One useful choice, several tempting detours

Schematic state-space structures, not actual Rush Hour instances. Both may have the same shortest solution distance, while a fallible solver takes much longer on B.

This is closely related to Jarušek and Pelánek's 2011 model, which combines random exploration with a preference for optimal moves. They assessed difficulty by repeatedly simulating traversal of the state graph and measuring the mean number of steps to reach the goal.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://cdn.aaai.org\&sz=32)

cdn.aaai.org



There is an important limitation: the policy above knows the true distance to the goal. Human players don't. It is a useful graph-structure baseline, but it may underestimate difficulty arising from hidden dependencies or counterintuitive moves.

For realistic difficulty, we should eventually replace \\(d^\*\\) in the action-selection policy with a heuristic that represents what a human player can reasonably perceive.

A useful qualification from the 2011 results: for their particular Rush Hour dataset, shortest-path length was already a strong predictor of human solving time (Pearson correlation 0.77; Spearman 0.90). Their probabilistic traversal model did not improve on it. The model added more value for Sokoban and the third puzzle. So shortest-path length should remain your baseline, and any more sophisticated measure should demonstrate that it improves prediction on your own levels.&#x20;

## 4. Measure reasoning complexity using blocker dependencies

This is where I'd invest most of the domain-specific work.

Rush Hour is naturally a backward-chaining problem:

> To move the target to the exit, clear its path. To clear the path, reposition blocking vehicles. To reposition those vehicles, clear their paths.

That produces an AND/OR dependency graph.

Target reaches exit

AND: Clear vehicle A and vehicle B

Move A

Requires clearing C

Move C

Available

Move B

OR: Up or down

Choose clearance strategy

One or more prerequisites

The reason to use both node types is important:

- AND: Both A and B must be cleared to let the target pass.
- OR: B might be cleared by moving it up or down. These alternatives can require very different preparatory moves.

Cycles are also interesting. For instance, clearing A may require moving B, but clearing B may require temporarily moving A elsewhere.

These cycles create situations where a player must plan a sequence of temporary configurations rather than a simple chain of forward progress.

### Research directly supporting this approach

The paper [A Computational Model of Backward Reasoning in Human Problem Solving](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42113-026-00335-2?utm_source=chatgpt.com) (Olieslagers and colleagues, published 25 September 2026) studies this exact approach using Rush Hour and Tower of London.

The researchers build AND/OR trees of subgoals and model how humans search those trees. In their study, roughly 80–90% of participants' moves were classified as sensible by the dependency representation. Players also tended to prefer actions that required shorter chains of reasoning.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://link.springer.com\&sz=32)

Springer Nature Link



This is potentially a better difficulty model for your game than a purely geometric measure.

I would extract these features:

| Feature                           | Interpretation                                             |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Maximum relevant dependency depth | How far ahead a player must reason                         |
| OR branching                      | Number of plausible clearance strategies                   |
| Number of required subgoals       | Planning workload                                          |
| Dependency cycles                 | Need for temporary rearrangements                          |
| Shared blockers                   | One piece participates in multiple interacting subgoals    |
| Counterintuitive moves            | A required move worsens an obvious visual progress measure |

Each should be measured on the actual blocker relationships, not merely on the number of vehicles.

A puzzle with 30 independent vehicles might have an enormous state space but little genuine reasoning difficulty. A puzzle with six tightly interdependent vehicles can be significantly more challenging.

## 5. Quantifying misleading decisions: regret and information

There are two additional metrics I'd compute from the state graph.

### A. Move regret

Define the regret of taking action \\(a\\) at state \\(s\\) as:

\\[ R(s,a)=1+d^\*(T(s,a))-d^\*(s) \\]

Because \\(d^\*\\) is the optimal remaining cost, regret is always nonnegative.

- Regret 0: The move can begin an optimal solution.
- Regret 1: The move increases the optimal solution length by one.
- Regret 2: The move increases it by two.

For any completed playthrough, a useful identity follows:

\\[ \boxed{\sum\_{a\text{ played}}R(s,a)=L\_{\text{actual}}-L\_{\text{optimal}}} \\]

This means all the additional moves a player makes can be attributed to choices with positive regret.

For puzzle generation, the interesting question is not merely how many moves have positive regret, but how attractive those moves appear to a player.

Imagine two moves:

- Moving A to the right visibly clears a corridor, but makes the actual solution longer.
- Moving B to the left looks pointless, but is the first step of an optimal solution.

That is a strong source of perceived difficulty.

A practical metric is the expected regret of the player's next choice:

\\[ \mathbb{E}\_{a\sim\pi}[R(s,a)] \\]

The policy \\(\pi\\) should ultimately be a model of a human player, not just a uniform distribution over legal moves.

### B. Decision information

Another approach is to quantify how much information a player needs to choose an optimal move.

Let \\(A^\*(s)\\) be the set of optimal actions and \\(\pi(a\mid s)\\) the probability that the player would choose \\(a\\).

Define:

\\[ I(s)=-\log_2\left(\sum\_{a\in A^\*(s)}\pi(a\mid s)\right) \\]

If the player has a 50% probability of choosing an optimal action, \\(I(s)=1\\) bit. If only one of eight equally likely moves is optimal, \\(I(s)=3\\) bits.

This is a simple proposed metric, not a validated universal measure.

It is closely related to established puzzle entropy research:

- [Entropy as a Measure of Puzzle Difficulty (2023)](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/27499?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
- [Generalized Entropy and Solution Information for Measuring Puzzle Difficulty (2024)](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/31872?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Those papers formulate difficulty in terms of the information needed to guide a player to a solution, taking the player's knowledge or policy into account. Their experiments focus on The Witness, but the general methodology is relevant to your game.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://ojs.aaai.org\&sz=32)

AAAI Journals+1



For Unblock Me, I would calculate decision information over meaningful strategies or clearance choices, not necessarily every possible stopping coordinate. Otherwise, a long unobstructed lane could artificially inflate the metric.

## 6. Which state transitions can you prune?

This needs a distinction between solving a puzzle and measuring its difficulty.

### For optimal solving

If the exact distance map is available, you only need actions satisfying:

\\[ d^\*(T(s,a))=d^\*(s)-1 \\]

to construct an optimal solution.

That is sound because every non-goal state with a finite shortest distance has at least one such successor.

However, when you don't know \\(d^\*\\), pruning actions because they fail to improve a heuristic is generally unsafe.

A heuristic like "number of vehicles blocking the exit" might increase temporarily along the optimal solution.

### For difficulty estimation

You must not automatically remove all non-optimal moves.

The difficulty of the puzzle may come precisely from the presence of those alternatives. Removing them from the player model would make a deceptive puzzle appear trivial.

I would therefore maintain two conceptual graphs:

| Graph                 | Used for                         | Allowed reductions                                                                     |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Solver graph          | Find/prove solutions efficiently | A\*, SSS/POR, abstractions, dominance                                                  |
| Player-decision graph | Estimate difficulty              | Only reductions that preserve relevant player choices or model their aggregate effects |

You don't necessarily need to store two physical graphs. The distinction can be implemented as different successor generators and different policies.

Strong stubborn sets preserve appropriate optimal planning objectives, but they don't generally preserve the original number of available choices or the difficulty perceived by an exploring player.&#x20;

## 7. Estimating difficulty from a subset of the state space

For larger levels, I would use a hierarchy of increasingly expensive estimators.

| Stage                                | States explored                     | Information obtained                               |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Static dependency analysis        | None, or a few local configurations | Blockers, clearance alternatives, dependency depth |
| 2. Relaxed puzzle / pattern database | Small projections                   | Lower bounds on solution cost                      |
| 3. Bounded A\*                       | Promising states only               | Candidate solution, cost bounds, search effort     |
| 4. Player-model rollouts             | Sampled trajectories                | Estimated solution time, mistakes, completion rate |
| 5. Exhaustive analysis               | Entire finite graph                 | Exact distances, global graph metrics, validation  |

### Pattern databases are particularly relevant

Suppose a puzzle contains 20 vehicles, but only five are currently part of the target's blocking dependency chain.

You can construct a smaller projected puzzle with those five vehicles and compute its state space exactly.

The resulting pattern database can provide a lower bound for the original puzzle, provided the projection relaxes constraints rather than adding new ones.

\\[ h\_{\mathrm{PDB}}(s)\leq d^\*(s) \\]

You can build several pattern databases for overlapping groups of interacting vehicles and take their maximum:

\\[ h(s)=\max_i h\_{\mathrm{PDB},i}(s) \\]

Taking the maximum preserves admissibility. Adding pattern database estimates requires more restrictive cost-partitioning conditions, since otherwise the same move may be counted multiple times.

This is a mature technique in optimal search, and related work has used pattern databases to evaluate and generate difficult Sokoban states.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://doi.org\&sz=32)

DOI+1



### A\* can return useful bounds before finishing

With an admissible heuristic:

\\[ f(s)=g(s)+h(s) \\]

A\* can explore only selected states while giving you useful information.

Once a solution of length \\(U\\) has been found, \\(U\\) is an upper bound on the optimal solution length. The minimum remaining frontier \\(f\\)-value provides a lower bound, assuming the usual correct graph-search bookkeeping.

Thus, you can obtain:

\\[ L\leq d^\*(s_0)\leq U \\]

without necessarily completing the search.

For generated puzzles, this allows you to reject candidates cheaply when their maximum possible difficulty is below your threshold.

### Estimate perceived difficulty by sampled player trajectories

Instead of enumerating the graph, run many simulated players with different reasoning capabilities.

Measure their completion rate within a move budget, number of moves taken, frequency of undoing moves, and number of times they revisit previously explored configurations.

For example, simulate one model that makes mostly goal-directed decisions, one that searches shallow AND/OR dependency trees, and one that explores more randomly.

For a candidate puzzle, the distribution of results can be much more informative than a single solver's node count.

You can allocate more simulations to candidates whose estimated scores are both high and uncertain. This is a form of adaptive sampling.

There is also empirical support for using solver statistics as inexpensive proxies: a 2025 study found that A\* closed-list size correlated with perceived difficulty and player movements in Sokoban, although that result should not be assumed to transfer unchanged to Rush Hour.&#x20;

## 8. How to generate increasingly difficult levels

I would use a generate-and-evaluate search over puzzle configurations.

For a fixed arrangement of vehicle identities, lengths, and lanes, Rush Hour has an especially useful property: moves are reversible. Starting from a solved configuration and exploring backward guarantees that every generated starting position is solvable.

Changing which vehicles exist or which lanes they occupy creates a different puzzle family, so generation has two levels.

Generate a vehicle layout

Orientations, lengths, lanes and target position

Generate solvable starting states

Reverse search from goal configurations

Cheap difficulty screening

Dependency depth, relaxed heuristics, novelty, clutter

Expensive evaluation of promising candidates

Bounded/exact solving, human-policy rollouts, alternative paths

Keep difficult, diverse, fair puzzles

Reject repetitive puzzles and unnecessary complexity

For exploring the space of layouts, I'd start with mutation plus beam search or simulated annealing. Mutations could move a vehicle to another lane, change its length, or add/remove a vehicle, subject to geometric validity.

For generating starts within a fixed layout, backward search is especially attractive because solvability is maintained by construction.

A very relevant example is [Procedural Generation of Initial States of Sokoban](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2019/646?utm_source=chatgpt.com) (Bento, Pereira & Lelis, 2019). Their Beta system combines backward search, pattern-database-based hardness measures, and novelty to generate difficult initial states. Their experiments found instances harder for a specialized solver than human-designed examples.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.ijcai.org\&sz=32)

ijcai.org



For your game, I would add a diversity mechanism so the generator doesn't simply produce hundreds of variations of the same blocker chain.

## 9. Combining everything with your infinite-board solver

Here is how I would assign roles to all the techniques you've mentioned.

| Technique                     | Solver role                                 | Generator/difficulty role                                     |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Critical-position abstraction | Reduce geometric successor generation       | Identify meaningful positions and clearance alternatives      |
| Symbolic position intervals   | Represent sets of coordinates compactly     | Describe ranges of geometrically similar choices              |
| Goal dependency analysis      | Find relevant actions and useful heuristics | Estimate human planning depth and blocker interaction         |
| Factored / decoupled search   | Avoid independent combinations              | Distinguish meaningful interactions from independent activity |
| Strong stubborn sets          | Prune redundant action orderings            | Avoid duplicate computation, not necessarily player choices   |
| A / Dijkstra\*                | Find shortest solutions                     | Get optimal length, bounds and search-effort metrics          |
| Pattern databases             | Lower-bound cost of interacting subproblems | Cheap hardness estimates                                      |
| Human-policy simulation       | Not required for optimal solving            | Estimate perceived difficulty and likely mistakes             |

Two implementation cautions are particularly important.

First, critical-position abstraction must preserve the move-cost model. If a piece can stop at any coordinate and every slide costs one move, retaining only collision endpoints is not automatically exact. Different intermediate stopping points can enable different future moves. You'll need either a proof that a coordinate class is equivalent for your objectives or a relaxed abstraction used only for lower bounds.

Second, an infinite board may have infinitely many legal slide destinations. Before defining a human difficulty metric, you need a model of what constitutes a meaningful player choice. A uniform random choice over infinitely many stopping coordinates is not defined. An event-based action representation—moving a vehicle to clear, block, or align with a relevant corridor—could be more appropriate.

For large levels, I would organize the search around the interaction frontier: the small set of vehicles and positions currently needed to satisfy goal-related subgoals. Local, independent movements can be handled by factors or symbolic representations, while the global search explores interactions and their necessary order.

This connects your earlier performance-optimization question with the new generation question: the same dependency structure used to accelerate solving can provide a meaningful source of human difficulty features.

## 10. A concrete difficulty score for a first implementation

I would initially retain separate measurements instead of immediately reducing everything to one number:

\\[ D(P)= \left( L^\*, H\_\pi, D\_{\mathrm{dep}}, I\_{\mathrm{choice}}, C\_{\mathrm{counter}}, N\_{\mathrm{relevant}} \right) \\]

where:

| Metric                       | Definition                                                     |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| \\(L^\*\\)                   | Optimal solution length                                        |
| \\(H\_\pi\\)                 | Expected or capped simulated player effort                     |
| \\(D\_{\mathrm{dep}}\\)      | Goal-dependency reasoning depth                                |
| \\(I\_{\mathrm{choice}}\\)   | Decision information on consequential choices                  |
| \\(C\_{\mathrm{counter}}\\)  | Number/severity of counterintuitive moves on optimal solutions |
| \\(N\_{\mathrm{relevant}}\\) | Number of vehicles genuinely involved in the solution          |

Then optimize a Pareto frontier instead of simply maximizing a weighted score.

For example, two puzzles might both be rated difficult, but one requires long, systematic planning and the other requires discovering a short but unintuitive dependency cycle. Those are different kinds of difficulty, and both may be worth generating.

When you have human play data, you can fit a model predicting actual solve time, completion probability, excess moves, or subjective difficulty from those features.

In a 2024 empirical study of commercial mobile puzzle games, models that combined player/cohort data with simulated agent statistics performed better than using the categories separately. This supports treating human calibration as a later layer on top of procedural analysis.&#x20;

## 11. Recommended implementation sequence

I would build this in four phases.

1\. Exact small-board analyzer. Generate the complete reachable state graph, calculate reverse BFS distances, store optimal actions, regret, and shortest solutions. This becomes the reference implementation for testing all optimizations.

2\. Domain-specific reasoning model. Construct blocker AND/OR graphs, compute planning-depth features, and simulate a player who searches those graphs. Compare its predicted difficulty with shortest-path length.

3\. Generation engine. Generate vehicle layouts, sample solvable start states by reverse search, and rank candidates by both difficulty and novelty. Run exact analysis only on promising candidates.

4\. Scalable implementation. Introduce pattern databases, critical-position and interval abstractions, goal dependencies, SSS, decoupled search, and bounded A\*. Compare every reduced result against the exact implementation on small boards to check that solution costs and required properties are preserved.

The most important architectural decision is to keep the optimal solver, player model, and generator separate but sharing the same puzzle geometry and state representation. That lets you make the solver increasingly aggressive without accidentally changing what the difficulty model believes a human can do.

# Further research: generating difficult Rush Hour / Unblock Me puzzles

I found several directly relevant papers that go beyond the research we discussed earlier. The most interesting discoveries are:

1. A Rush Hour-specific level generator that uses blocker-dependency analysis and neural networks.
2. A 2026 paper on searching Rush Hour configurations using compact state representations and clusters.
3. Research on using large-scale search to generate puzzles, rather than merely solving them.

These suggest that your generator could eventually optimize three different properties simultaneously: minimum solution length, difficulty of discovering the solution, and the extent to which the puzzle's pieces interact meaningfully.

## 1. Research specifically about Rush Hour generation

[Rush Hour puzzle solver and generator – FlowingData](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/_EhQGAfhwHySW0rXP6LoZUNCxgGCTkzK3CCS7dcyTixVDHTSxAPX1fSjxkaf1vNaX6WjxZ3oYv6zNDGYg5NIF3WHS_vKTPYI6k8kQTh2PeouSvCrAy6eSJYR_B8nM7b66UuEUCjLWJvgpX5y31fLid-QqW4Ow15b3dAIGuBkKSo?purpose=inline)

[Procedural Generation of Rush Hour Levels](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cazenave/papers/RushHour.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

de Batz de Trenquelléon et al. · CG 2022 / published 2023

This is probably the most directly applicable implementation reference. It combines a Rush Hour solver, a classifier for solvability, and a classifier for difficulty, tested on 6×6 and 7×7 boards.

Their solver introduces a particularly relevant geometric heuristic:

- Identify vehicles directly blocking the target's escape.
- Repeatedly add vehicles blocking those vehicles.
- Continue until the dependency set stops growing.
- Use the number of vehicles in the set as a heuristic score.

This is essentially a goal-relevant blocker dependency closure—the kind of structure we've been discussing.

Their greedy best-first solver improved solution-finding performance over BFS, while their neural classifier was used to screen candidates. They reported 87.5% classification accuracy for their difficulty categories, with difficulty defined by optimal solution length—not experimentally measured human difficulty.&#x20;

Why it matters: You have a concrete Rush Hour implementation of dependency-based heuristic search to use as a baseline.

## 2. A 2026 paper that is almost exactly your problem

## 3. New difficulty research using A\* behavior

[Modeling Puzzle Difficulty via A\* Search Behavior, Solution Paths, and Spatial Metrics](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/408441841_Modeling_Puzzle_Difficulty_via_A_Search_Behavior_Solution_Paths_and_Spatial_Metrics?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Schaa, Quiroz & Barriga · July 2026 · Preprint

This paper evaluates difficulty metrics across Sokoban, Safari Rush Hour, and Asteroid Escape.

Instead of using only solution length, it analyzes properties of the search process.

| Metric family       | Examples                                                      |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Search effort       | A\* expanded states, generated successors, closed-list size   |
| Branching structure | Average, minimum and maximum effective branching              |
| Solution trajectory | Successors and repeated states encountered along the solution |
| Spatial properties  | Board dimensions, density and obstacle arrangements           |

An interesting finding is that lower effective branching factors correlated with greater perceived difficulty, while greater total search effort generally correlated positively with difficulty.

For Safari Rush Hour, search-effort and solution-path metrics often achieved moderate to strong positive correlations with difficulty ratings (roughly Spearman \\(\rho=0.4\\)–\\(0.65\\)).&#x20;

### A new metric I'd investigate

One research direction that I didn't find established as a standard Rush Hour metric is interaction complexity along optimal solutions.

You could construct a causal dependency graph for each meaningful phase of a solution and measure the changes in this graph as the puzzle progresses.

For example, consider:

\\[ C(P)= \min\_{\pi\in\operatorname{Opt}(P)} \sum\_{k=0}^{|\pi|-1} \operatorname{Complexity}\bigl(D(s_k)\bigr) \\]

where \\(D(s_k)\\) is the goal-relevant dependency graph at state \\(s_k\\).

Taking the minimum over optimal solutions matters: a puzzle shouldn't be judged difficult merely because one unnecessarily complicated solution exists.

The complexity function could incorporate dependency depth, interactions between otherwise independent components, and the number of alternative subgoal decompositions.

This would be a proposed research metric, not an existing validated formula. It might help identify puzzles where the reasoning structure itself is difficult, rather than the graph merely being large.

It also connects naturally to your infinite-board optimizations: the dependency graph can be much smaller than the state space, and calculating its complexity doesn't necessarily require enumerating all legal positions.

### What I'd prioritize reading

Of everything found, I would put the two July 2026 preprints first, followed by Gestalt Effects in Planning (2014) and Data-Driven Sokoban Puzzle Generation with MCTS (2016).

Together, these four cover the central challenges:

- Represent and explore a large family of levels efficiently.
- Estimate difficulty from partial search information.
- Account for misleading visual structure and human reasoning.
- Search the space of solvable levels to generate diverse, interesting configurations.

One potentially valuable experiment would be to compare three different notions of puzzle hardness on the same set of generated boards: optimal solution length, A\* effort on the unpruned graph, and dependency-graph complexity. Then test which combination best predicts human solving time. This would establish whether your domain-specific structure offers predictive value beyond existing search-based metrics.
